Sizing a bankroll when you don't know your edge

Bankroll formulas take your win rate as an input. Your win rate is an estimate with a wide interval, and the answer moves a lot when the input does.

Risk of ruin

Treat each hand as a draw with mean μ and standard deviation σ, both in big blinds per hand. The chance of ever losing a bankroll of B big blinds is approximately

RoR = exp(−2μB / σ²)

Solve for B and you get the bankroll for a target risk r:

B = −σ² ln(r) / (2μ)

A 5 bb/100 winner with a standard deviation of 90 bb/100 has μ = 0.05 and σ = 9 per hand. Standard deviation grows with the square root of the number of hands, so 90 / √100 = 9. That gives:

When the edge is wrong

The win-rate note showed that after 50,000 hands, a measured +5 bb/100 is consistent with anything from about −3 to +13. Suppose you size for 5% risk with 24 buy-ins and your true rate is lower:

Halving the edge raises the risk of ruin more than fourfold.

The Kelly version

Kelly sizing asks the same question as a growth rate. For a repeated bet that returns mean μ and standard deviation σ per unit staked, staking a fraction f of the bankroll grows it at roughly

g(f) = μf − σ²f² / 2

per bet, which peaks at f* = μ / σ². In poker the stake is the limit you play relative to your bankroll, so moving up a limit raises f. Two facts fall out of the formula:

  • Staking twice the Kelly fraction gives a growth rate of zero. If you size for a 5 bb/100 edge and your real edge is 2.5, that is exactly what you are doing.
  • Staking half the Kelly fraction keeps 75% of the growth rate with a quarter of the variance.

The second fact is why people bet fractional Kelly. It is cheap insurance against an edge that turns out smaller than the estimate.

What I take from it

Size from the low end of the interval rather than the point estimate. The same rule decides whether a model gets deployed. In When Average Effects Lie, targeting is recommended for the Women's email, where the bootstrap interval for the gain stays above zero, and not for the Men's email, where it straddles zero.
