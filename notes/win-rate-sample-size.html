How many hands until a win rate means something?

A poker win rate is a sample mean with a huge standard deviation. Before you can say you beat a game, you need far more hands than most players expect.

The setup

Results in no-limit hold'em cash games are quoted in big blinds won per 100 hands (bb/100). For a 6-max player, the standard deviation of that number is commonly quoted at 80 to 100 bb/100. I'll use 90.

Over n hands you have n/100 blocks of 100 hands, so the standard error of your observed win rate is

SE = σ / √(n / 100)

and a 95% confidence interval is the observed rate plus or minus 1.96 × SE.

What that looks like

Say you are a real 5 bb/100 winner, which is a good rate. Here is the 95% interval around that +5 at different sample sizes, with σ = 90:

HandsStandard error95% interval (bb/100)
10,0009.0−12.6 to +22.6
50,0004.0−2.9 to +12.9
100,0002.8−0.6 to +10.6
250,0001.8+1.5 to +8.5
1,000,0000.9+3.2 to +6.8

At 10,000 hands, which is a few months of regular play, the data can't tell a strong winner from someone losing 12 bb/100.

The two numbers worth knowing

For the interval around a true win rate w to clear zero, you need

n = 100 × (1.96 × σ / w)²

For w = 5 and σ = 90, that is about 124,000 hands.

That number has a catch. If your true rate is exactly 5 and you play exactly 124,000 hands, your observed rate is as likely to land below 5 as above it, so the interval clears zero only half the time. To clear it 80% of the time, which is the usual bar for statistical power, add 0.84 to the 1.96:

n = 100 × ((1.96 + 0.84) × σ / w)²

That comes to about 254,000 hands.

Why I care outside poker

The same arithmetic decides whether a backtest or an A/B test means anything. A strategy that looks good over a short window is a 10,000-hand sample. That is why When Average Effects Lie puts a bootstrap interval on every dollar figure, and why the NYC Collisions backtest checks its ranking against a permutation null before calling it predictive.
