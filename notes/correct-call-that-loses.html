A correct call that loses 70% of the time

Poker players call it resulting: judging a decision by how it turned out. Here is how badly that misleads in a spot where the right play loses most of the time.

The spot

On the river the pot is 10 big blinds and your opponent bets 5. Calling costs 5 to win 15, so the call breaks even if you win 5 / 20 = 25% of the time. Say your hand wins 30% against the range they bet with.

EV(call) = 0.30 × 15 − 0.70 × 5 = +1.0 bb

Calling is correct. It also loses 7 times out of 10.

How often the right play shows a loss

Make the same call n times. With W wins you net

15W − 5(n − W) = 20W − 5n

which is below zero whenever you win fewer than a quarter of the calls. W is binomial with p = 0.3, so:

CallsExpected resultChance you're down
20+20 bb24%
100+100 bb11%
500+500 bb0.6%

Someone who makes this call 20 times and judges it by the result has about a one-in-four chance of deciding that calling is a mistake.

Grade the decision

The useful question is whether the call had positive expected value given what you knew, and that question has an answer as soon as you make the call. The poker trainer shows the EV of every alternative in a spot, and LineLab's Arena grades each decision against the EV of the other moves, whatever happened next.

The same mistake with models

A model that wins one backtest window, or a campaign that has one good week, is the sample of 20 calls. Hall Check's forecast only ships if it beats simple baselines on a held-out stretch of time, and the NYC Collisions backtest reports where its ranking only ties a plain injury-rate sort. Both are ways of judging the process on enough samples before trusting a result.
